Our method

How we grade the evidence

Pet advice is full of confident claims that contradict each other. So every claim in a Pet Primer article gets a grade that tells you how much evidence stands behind it, in plain words, with the sources linked underneath.

The five grades

StrongSeveral good studies or long-established veterinary science agree, and there is little serious doubt.
ModerateReal evidence supports it, but with limits: a single large survey, owner-reported data, or studies that haven’t been repeated much.
EarlyA sensible idea with some early support, such as a small study, a theory from related animals, or results that haven’t been tested directly yet.
WeakMostly anecdote, or the evidence is mixed and leans against it.
Not supportedThe evidence points against it. Myth checks with this verdict are popular beliefs that research does not back up.

A grade describes the evidence, not how important the advice is. “Grapes can poison dogs” is graded Strong because it is well documented, and it is also urgent. A harmless habit can have a Weak grade simply because nobody has studied it.

Which sources count most

  • Research in veterinary and animal science journals, especially reviews that pull many studies together.
  • Veterinary reference works and vet school guidance, such as the Merck Veterinary Manual and university veterinary hospitals.
  • Poison control and public health data, for example the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center and the US FDA.
  • Large owner surveys, which are useful for everyday behaviour but rely on what owners notice, so they rarely earn more than Moderate on their own.
  • Personal experience, including mine. It is clearly labelled as experience and never graded as proof.

How an article is checked

  • Each article is split into individual claims, so one shaky point doesn’t hide behind a solid one.
  • Every graded claim lists its sources so you can read them yourself.
  • The date at the top shows when the article was last reviewed.
  • When new research changes the picture, the grade changes too.

What a grade is not

Pet Primer is not a vet and a grade is not a diagnosis. Every pet is different. If your pet may be unwell or in danger, contact a veterinarian or a pet poison helpline straight away.

If you think a grade is wrong or a better study exists, email petprimer.care@gmail.com with the link. Corrections are welcome and get reviewed.
